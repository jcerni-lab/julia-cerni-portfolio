iOS Photos App Redesign

Project details

  • Timeline: 3 weeks
  • Role: UX Researcher & Designer — user interviews, task analysis, usability and accessibility evaluation
  • Team: Myself, Aleyda Casillas, John Mosley, Saskia Mattiko, Benjamin Kettor Jr, Chris Shang
  • Methods: Interviews, heuristic analysis, master apprentice model
  • Tools: interview notes, hand sketched interface concepts, design frameworks
  • Focus: Accessibility, usability, and human-centered design

Context

This project explored how users interact with photo apps and where current designs fail to support organization, accessibility, and user control. Through interviews, observation, and heuristic evaluation, our team identified recurring pain points related to photo overload and navigation. I focused on translating these findings into clearer organizational systems and more intuitive user flows.

Brainstorming

Early brainstorming revealed repeated user frustration following a recent update. Changes to organization and navigation disrupted users’ existing mental models, making it difficult to locate and manage photos efficiently. This made the app a compelling case for examining how design updates impact usability and accessibility.

Research and Data Collection

We conducted structured user interviews paired with observational task analysis to understand how people navigate and organize photos in the iOS Photos app. Participants used their own devices while completing common tasks, allowing us to observe breakdowns in navigation, organization, and user control. We followed a master–apprentice approach, letting users lead while we observed moments of confusion and hesitation.

Research Structure

  • Pre-task questions to understand participants’ photo usage habits, organizational strategies, and prior frustrations
  • Task-based observation to identify breakdowns in navigation, control, and discoverability
  • Post-task reflection to capture perceived effort, confusion, and expectations around organization and control

Error Analysis- How can these be addressed?

Across seventeen user interviews and task-based observations, recurring errors emerged as participants navigated and organized content within the iOS Photos app. Using Norman’s framework, these errors were categorized.

Mode Errors

Mode errors occurred when users assumed consistency between different views within the app, particularly between the Library and Collections screens (Figures 2A and 2B). Participants often expected actions available in one view to behave similarly in another. When this expectation was not met, users became disoriented and unsure how to proceed, frequently navigating back and forth between screens in an attempt to reorient themselves. These inconsistencies disrupted users’ mental models and increased cognitive load during otherwise routine tasks.

Knowledge Based Mistakes

Knowledge-based mistakes were the most common errors observed and stemmed from users’ uncertainty about how the app organizes and manages photos. A clear example involved album creation. Multiple participants struggled to locate the album creation feature and initially believed the app did not support this function. When the feature was revealed under the Share icon, users expressed surprise, as they associated this icon with exporting or sending content rather than organizing photos. This mismatch between design cues and user expectations led to hesitation, repeated searching, and reliance on trial-and-error.

Description-Based Slips

Description-based slips occurred when users misinterpreted icons or labels that did not clearly communicate their function. In several instances, participants selected options based on assumed meanings that differed from the system’s actual behavior. These slips resulted in users initiating unintended actions or abandoning tasks due to uncertainty about the outcomes of their selections.

Capture Slips

Capture slips were observed when users relied on familiar interaction patterns learned from other applications. During repetitive actions such as photo deletion or navigation, participants occasionally defaulted to habitual gestures that did not align with the Photos app’s workflow. These slips highlight how inconsistencies across platforms can interfere with learned behaviors and contribute to user error.

Patterns and Key Findings

Overall, 12/17 of interviewees use this app on a daily basis, and the majority of the others use it multiple times a week. One common issue with the design of the app is accidentally performing functions such as deleting, sharing, or editing. This is represented by the 37.5% of interviewees occasionally if not more experiencing this issue. Every interviewee selected that they use their photo app to view photos, 70% use it to share as well, and 40% to edit their photos. With a large number of users utilizing the share feature, the issues analyzed with this feature are a trend observed. To be specific, the “shared album feature is really confusing and complicated” (interviewee 11). The editing features also tend to have varied confusions, as “the extra editing options feel a bit hidden”(interviewee 12). When asked what features they would change, one interviewee replied  “the signage for editing, filtering, drawing and cropping” (interviewee 14). Also, when asked regarding redesign, multiple users reported that they would appreciate less clutter and confusion regarding the editing process.

Overall, a trend/pattern that could allow for less confusions regarding sharing would be to address the amount of users that accidentally perform functions such as deleting, sharing, or editing. This is caused by lack of clear signifiers, problems with the select tool being one of them, and the overall lack of organization of the app and each category of actions to take. 

Design Space

Conducting interviews gave us a lot of interesting directions to head forward in, but in order to fully understand the depth of the issue, we engaged in the iterative design process known as “The Double Diamond” for the redesign of the iPhone Photos App. Moving from our broad pool of intuitions, we decided to quantify our qualitative data down to narrow our observed trends down to specific design tradeoffs. This allowed us to specify design space axes and deliver a focused and well justified prototype.  Using the comparative space and analyzing other common design trends in the market, we were able to tailor our design to match observed user trends to create the final design.

Tradeoffs

Our interviews revealed two major design trade-offs within the iPhone Photos app that directly.

Simplicity vs Navigability

The first major  tradeoff arises from Apple’s emphasis on a clean, minimalist interface. While this simplicity makes the app feel approachable and uncluttered, it also hides important tools and reduces the visibility of key features. Throughout our interviews, this tension appeared repeatedly. 11 participants described the app as generally “easy” or “intuitive,” but when asked to perform tasks beyond basic scrolling, many of them began to hesitate. 7 users struggled to create a new album without experimenting or accidentally opening the wrong menu, and 5 pointed out specific sections such as the editing toolbar, the albums page, and the “Recently Deleted” folder as confusing or hard to locate. Even distinguishing photos from videos was not universally straightforward, despite the small indicator Apple uses; several users emphasized that they had to look closely or rely on trial and error.

This trade-off shows how simplicity can reduce cognitive load while increasing the likelihood of slips. Because the interface hides signifiers in favor of visual cleanliness, users often overscroll, miss tools, or abandon helpful features entirely. A cleaner layout helps with quick browsing, but it restricts discoverability and limits the system’s ability to communicate knowledge in the world. Many users ended up relying heavily on their own memory and experience because the interface provided too little visible guidance within each task.

Automation vs User Control

Another major tradeoff we identified concerns the balance between automation and user

control. The Photos app attempts to support users by automatically categorizing images, identifying faces, generating “For You” albums, and producing suggested grouping such as wallpapers or memories. While these automated features are intended to save time, they frequently fall short of user expectations. Several participants noted that search results were inaccurate or incomplete, and this issue caused them to not trust automated organizations. 5 users admitted that they rarely organize their photos at all because the existing tools felt hidden or difficult to use. Others expressed annoyance with automatically generated albums that cluttered the interface rather than helping them stay organized.

This tension often pushes users back toward manual scrolling or trial-and-error searching strategies that are slower and more error-prone. When automation fails, users lose confidence in the system and revert to less efficient methods. However, when relying entirely on manual control, they face the burden of navigating long timelines, creating albums, and deleting photos one by one. Automation is efficient but imperfect; manual control is accurate but too time consuming. As a result, neither extreme fully supports user needs, and several participants explicitly requested features like improved duplicate detection, more transparent auto grouping, and better multi-select tools.

Redesign Overview

Based on interview findings and design space analysis, our redesign focuses on improving organization, discoverability, and user control while preserving the simplicity users value in iOS Photos. The goal was not to rebuild the app, but to reduce friction caused by hidden actions and limited customization.

This placement reflects our goal of supporting user agency alongside intelligent system assistance.

Redesign Overview

To address key issues with organization and multi-photo actions such as sharing and deleting, we introduced a new feature called the Photo Manager. This feature appears as a bar at the top of the app and opens a dedicated workspace for managing photos. Within this view, users can swipe right to delete and left to keep photos, with an Edit option that allows swipe actions to be customized.

The Edit mode enables users to assign different functions to left and right swipes, including share, save to album, AirDrop, delete, save, group, hide, paste or copy edits, and favorite. Once finished, users tap a Finish button at the bottom of the screen, which prompts confirmation options.

By long-pressing the screen, users can enter an editing mode that allows them to add or remove functions at any time, improving flexibility and control.

Lofi redesign

This low-fidelity redesign introduces a Photo Manager entry point that makes key actions—such as organizing, editing collections, and managing batch actions—more visible. This structure reduces reliance on hidden menus and aligns better with users’ mental models without adding unnecessary clutter.

Redesign Tradeoffs

To improve clarity and control, the redesign accepts a small increase in interface complexity. However, this trade-off was intentional: users consistently preferred clear options over minimal but opaque controls. The final design prioritizes reducing accidental actions and confusion while maintaining the app’s core functionality.

Conclusion

This project showed that many user errors in the iOS Photos app stem from hidden actions and limited user control rather than user inexperience. Our redesign introduces a Photo Manager mode and clearer signifiers to reduce accidental actions while maintaining simplicity. These changes better align the app with users’ mental models and everyday workflows.
