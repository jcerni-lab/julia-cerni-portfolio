Project Summary

This project analyzes the “host effect” in the Olympic Games to determine whether countries perform better when hosting compared to non-host years. Using the 120 Years of Olympic History dataset, we cleaned and structured the data and examined trends in total medals, gold medals, and athlete participation from 1896–2016. Our analysis revealed that host countries consistently earn more total medals and often more gold medals during their hosting year. These patterns suggest that factors such as home advantage, reduced travel fatigue, and increased national investment may contribute to improved performance.

Research Question and Hypothesis

How does the Olympic medal performance of host countries differ between the years they host the Games and the years they do not? We expect that countries hosting the Olympic Games tend to have better quantity and quality of performance, winning more medals and a greater share of gold medals compared to years when they are not the host nation. This may occur because hosting provides several advantages such as familiar environments, local crowd support, reduced travel fatigue, and better access to training facilities.

Data Preparation and Sources

Original Data Source: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/heesoo37/120-years-of-olympic-history-athletes-and-results

We prepared the Olympic dataset by organizing the raw data, examining its structure, and addressing missing values and outliers. Missing medal entries were labeled as “No Medal” to preserve all athlete records, while missing demographic values (age, height, and weight) were imputed using median values to maintain representative distributions. To support our analysis, we created a new variable identifying whether an athlete competed for a host country by mapping Olympic host years to their corresponding national committees. The final cleaned dataset was then exported for exploratory and statistical analysis.

Ethics

This project recognizes potential sources of bias in Olympic performance data, including differences in national funding, population size, and access to training resources, which may influence medal outcomes beyond athletic performance alone. To avoid misleading interpretations, results were contextualized carefully and limitations were clearly acknowledged. The dataset consists of publicly available historical records and does not contain sensitive personal information, minimizing privacy concerns. All analyses and visualizations were transparently labeled and reproducible through documented code and version control, supporting responsible and honest data communication.

Conclusion

Our analysis provides consistent evidence that host countries tend to achieve higher medal counts during the years they host the Olympic Games compared to non-host years. Across the exploratory figures, host nations generally outperform their own historical baselines, both in total medals and in gold medal quantities. This suggests that hosting may offer competitive advantages due to increased funding, home crowd support, familiarity with facilities, no long-distance travel, automatic qualification, and higher participation.

Future Work

Future work could address these limitations by using various statistical models that could factor in GDP, population and historical performance to show a more causal host effect. Additional analyses could examine whether home advantage effect is proportional to team sizes based on sports. It would also be valuable to analyze medal shares or per-athlete/per-event success rates rather than raw counts, which could help remove the impact of larger team sizes from genuine performance improvements.
